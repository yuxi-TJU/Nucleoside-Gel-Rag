The target molecule is isoguanosine (isoG), an isomer of guanosine in which the positions of the exocyclic amino and carbonyl groups on the nucleobase are swapped. This structural change alters the hydrogen-bonding pattern and promotes formation of different supramolecular assemblies (typically octameric rather than tetrameric G-quartets).

Reference data for the closest structural analog, 2′-deoxy-2′-fluoroisoguanosine (FisoGd), show that this compound produces transparent, mechanically stable hydrogels in 0.2 M LiCl under essentially identical concentration and thermal protocols (1.4 mg/100 µL, heat-cool). The gels remain intact for up to two months. Because the only difference between isoG and FisoGd is the 2′-OH versus 2′-F substituent on an otherwise identical isoguanine nucleoside, the same LiCl-driven assembly pathway is expected to operate for isoG.

In contrast, native guanosine (G) under the same LiCl conditions yields only transient hydrogels that collapse within minutes; the isoguanine scaffold therefore overrides the instability observed with G. Consequently, isoG in 0.2 M LiCl is predicted to produce a stable hydrogel.
