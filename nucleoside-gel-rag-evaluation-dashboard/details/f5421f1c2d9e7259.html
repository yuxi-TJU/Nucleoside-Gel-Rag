To determine the gelation potential of 2′,3′,5′-tri-O-acetylguanosine (TAcG), we must consider the structural requirements for guanosine-based supramolecular gelation. 

### Analysis
Guanosine derivatives typically form gels through the self-assembly of G-quartets, which are stabilized by Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding. A critical requirement for this assembly is the presence of free hydroxyl groups (specifically at the 2', 3', and 5' positions) to facilitate the necessary intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network and the coordination of cations (like $K^+$) within the G-quartet stack.

In the case of **2′,3′,5′-tri-O-acetylguanosine (TAcG)**, all three hydroxyl groups on the ribose sugar are protected by acetyl groups. This modification serves two purposes:
1. **Steric Hindrance:** The bulky acetyl groups prevent the close packing required for the formation of stable G-quartet columns.
2. **Loss of H-bonding Sites:** The absence of free hydroxyl groups eliminates the ability of the ribose moiety to participate in the secondary hydrogen-bonding interactions that typically stabilize the supramolecular architecture of guanosine gels.

Experimental literature on guanosine derivatives confirms that while guanosine and many of its derivatives with free hydroxyls form robust gels in the presence of salts like KCl, the per-acetylated derivative (TAcG) lacks the necessary structural features to undergo this self-assembly process. Consequently, TAcG remains soluble or precipitates depending on the solvent, but it does not form a supramolecular gel.
